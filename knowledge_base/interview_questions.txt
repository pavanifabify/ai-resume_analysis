Interview Preparation Guide

Types of Technical Interviews
1. Coding/DSA rounds - data structures, algorithms, problem-solving under time pressure.
2. System design rounds - architecture, scalability, trade-offs (usually for 2+ years
   experience, but entry-level system design is increasingly common at larger companies).
3. Behavioral rounds - past experiences, teamwork, conflict resolution, using
   frameworks like STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result).
4. Take-home assignments - a scoped project to assess real-world coding ability and
   code quality, not just algorithmic speed.

Preparing for Coding Rounds
- Practice explaining your thought process out loud, not just arriving at the answer.
  Interviewers weight communication and approach heavily, not just correctness.
- Cover core topics: arrays/strings, hashmaps, trees/graphs, recursion/DP, sorting/
  searching, and time/space complexity analysis.
- Practice on a whiteboard or plain text editor occasionally, since many interviews
  don't offer autocomplete or syntax highlighting.

Preparing for Behavioral Rounds (STAR Method)
- Situation: briefly set the context.
- Task: what were you responsible for?
- Action: what did YOU specifically do (not "we")?
- Result: what was the measurable or observed outcome, and what did you learn?
Prepare 5-6 flexible stories covering: a conflict you resolved, a failure you learned
from, a time you led without formal authority, a time you dealt with ambiguity, and a
proud technical accomplishment.

Questions to Ask the Interviewer
Strong candidates ask thoughtful questions that show genuine interest: "What does
success look like in this role after 6 months?", "What's the biggest technical
challenge the team is facing right now?", "How does the team handle code review and
technical debt?" Avoid questions answerable by a 30-second look at the company website.

Resume-to-Interview Consistency
Every bullet point on your resume is a potential interview question - be ready to go
deep on any project or achievement you list. Interviewers frequently probe claimed
metrics ("how did you measure that 40% improvement?") - be prepared to explain your
methodology, not just restate the number. Do not list a skill you cannot discuss
competently for at least a few minutes; being caught unable to explain a "skill" listed
on your resume damages credibility more than not listing it at all.

Salary Negotiation Basics
- Research market rate for the role, location, and experience level before any
  numbers are discussed.
- Let the employer state a number first when possible.
- If given a range, negotiate toward the top of the range, not the middle.
- Consider the full package (base, bonus, equity, benefits), not just base salary.
